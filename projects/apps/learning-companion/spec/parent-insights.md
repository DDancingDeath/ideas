# Parent visibility and learner discovery


## 4.11 Parent dashboard and child development profile

The parent experience should be informative, calm and actionable.

It should answer five questions:

1. Is my child learning?
2. Is the syllabus on track?
3. Where are they struggling?
4. What should happen next?
5. What interests or strengths are emerging?

All numbers and student information above are illustrative, not actual learner data.
Curriculum progress and retention checks are separate indicators with separate
denominators; the example does not equate them. Activity descriptions are not
additional [learning states](./learner-and-curriculum.md#coverage-versus-mastery).

### Required metrics

- Planned versus completed lessons.
- Curriculum coverage.
- Demonstrated mastery.
- Retention-confirmed mastery.
- Concepts needing review.
- Progress against the target date.
- Recent assessment performance.
- Study consistency.
- Confidence in each estimate.
- Recommended next steps.

Never display a single percentage as though it represents complete knowledge of the subject.

### Parent alerts

Send a notification only when it can help the parent act.

Examples:

- The study plan is becoming unrealistic.
- A foundational gap is blocking progress.
- A scheduled revision has repeatedly been missed.
- The child has made meaningful progress on a difficult concept.
- An exam milestone needs attention.

Avoid notifications that pressure parents to force the child to study or turn every missed session into a failure.

## 4.12 Discovering interests, strengths and future directions

Build a long-term, evidence-based picture of enjoyment and demonstrated
strengths. Academic mastery, personal interest and future aptitude must be
treated as three separate things.

A child might enjoy astronomy without being especially strong in physics yet. Another might be good at mathematics but have no interest in pursuing it. Both are perfectly normal.

### What the system should observe

- Topics explored voluntarily.
- Follow-up questions asked.
- Optional challenges attempted.
- Persistence after encountering difficulty.
- Types of problems the child enjoys.
- Ability to explain or apply a concept.
- Improvement after instruction.
- Interest across different contexts.
- Whether curiosity persists over multiple sessions.

Report qualitative evidence and confidence rather than invented numerical aptitude scores.

### How the system should make recommendations

Instead of saying:

> Your child should become an engineer.

Say:

> Your child has repeatedly explored how machines work, enjoyed mechanical puzzles, and shown growing skill in multi-step problem solving. You could offer age-appropriate engineering activities and see whether this interest continues.

The product can then recommend:

- A science experiment.
- A coding puzzle.
- A nature exploration.
- A design challenge.
- A visit to a museum or science center.
- A conversation with someone working in a field of interest.

Recommendations should broaden possibilities, not narrow them.

### Guardrails

- Never make a career recommendation from a single lesson.
- Do not rank siblings against one another.
- Do not treat slow progress as low potential.
- Avoid gender, income, family occupation or other demographic stereotypes.
- Do not claim to infer intelligence, personality or psychological traits from chat behavior.
- Let the child explore interests outside the parent's expectations.
- Allow parents to view, correct and delete relevant profile information.
- Keep future-path suggestions optional and age-appropriate.

A parent's profession may be a voluntarily chosen example context, not a basis
for assumptions about a child's interests or ability.
